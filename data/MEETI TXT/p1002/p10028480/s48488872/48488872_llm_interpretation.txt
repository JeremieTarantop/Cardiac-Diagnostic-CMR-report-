Upon examining the 12-lead ECG, certain key observations and abnormalities are apparent. There is evidence of atrial fibrillation characterized by irregular RR intervals and absent P waves, observable in multiple leads. This irregular rhythm supports the diagnosis of atrial fibrillation. The presence of a leftward QRS axis suggests left axis deviation, which can be corroborated through a predominantly negative QRS complex in Lead aVF and positive in Lead I. Inferior Q waves are visible in leads II, III, and aVF, indicating a pattern consistent with an inferior myocardial infarction of uncertain age. Notably, the QRS complex changes in leads V3 and V4 hint at possible left ventricular hypertrophy, although the possibility of anterior infarction cannot be completely excluded. This is due to the increased amplitude and duration of the QRS complexes in these leads. Additionally, there are secondary repolarization abnormalities, noticeably in the form of flattened or inverted T waves in leads V5 and V6, and non-specific ST-T wave changes laterally could be due to left ventricular hypertrophy or ischemia. Moreover, low QRS voltages in the precordial leads suggest diminished electrical activity, potentially related to pericardial effusion or significant myocardial damage. In evaluating the entire ECG, these abnormal findings align with expected manifestations of atrial fibrillation accompanying structural heart changes, an inferior infarction possibly due to a previous myocardial event, and likely left ventricular hypertrophy, potentially with ischemic changes. The comprehensive assessment of these ECG findings underpins an abnormal ECG diagnosis reflecting these pathophysiological conditions.
