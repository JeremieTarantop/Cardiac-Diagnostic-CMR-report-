Analysis of the QRS complexes across various leads indicates a left axis deviation, which correlates with the findings of increased QRS amplitudes and prolonged durations in leads such as V4 and V5. Such patterns are suggestive of possible left ventricular hypertrophy. The presence of inferior and lateral ST-T changes was noted, characterized by the upslope and declination forms in various leads, such as the ST segments in leads I, V2, and aVL. These changes in combination with the QRS findings may indicate that the ST-T alterations are secondary to ventricular hypertrophy rather than acute ischemia. Additionally, the borderline prolonged QT intervals seen particularly in leads like I and V5 warrant consideration, although they may be less concerning without accompanying evidence of torsades de pointes or syncope. T wave inversions or significant ST segment deviations, which could suggest a recent cardiac event, were not noted as prominently. All these findings cumulatively point towards sinus bradycardia with sinus arrhythmia, borderline first-degree A-V block, left axis deviation, and possible ventricular hypertrophy. While these findings indicate some non-urgent electrical activity alterations and structural heart considerations, the absence of acute ischemic changes suggests a chronic adaptation, likely due to ventricular hypertrophic processes. The ECG is deemed abnormal, but immediate intervention may not be necessary unless corroborated with clinical symptoms.
