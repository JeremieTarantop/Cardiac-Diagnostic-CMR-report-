Upon examining the 12-lead ECG image and evaluating the computed measurements, several findings contribute to a comprehensive clinical interpretation. The rhythm is sinus as indicated by a consistent P wave morphology preceding each QRS complex. The heart rate is within normal limits at 93 beats per minute. However, particular attention is drawn to the inferior leads (II, III, aVF), where significant findings are evident. In lead III and aVF, the machine measurements reveal diminished QRS amplitudes and a pattern suggestive of old inferior infarction characterized by the presence of abnormal Q waves. This pattern, combined with our understanding of myocardial blood supply, supports the diagnosis of an inferior myocardial infarction, which is chronic rather than acute.

The examination of the QT interval across multiple leads, such as lead I with a QTc interval exceeding the upper normal limit (averaging around 498 ms across heartbeats), confirms the presence of QT prolongation. This can be clinically significant as it may predispose the patient to arrhythmias. Additionally, the computed PR intervals largely fall within normal ranges, indicating no major conduction block issues.

ST segment analyses across the ECG further reveal no acute elevation or depression; however, some leads display ST segment changes suggestive of pre-existing structural heart changes rather than acute ischemia. Collectively, the absence of ST elevation or significant T wave inversion in the anterior and lateral leads argues against recent myocardial injury in these regions. Therefore, the cohesive interpretation of these key ECG features and machine measurements suggests a clinical diagnosis of sinus rhythm with an old inferior infarction and prolonged QT interval, consistent with the overall ECG presentation.
